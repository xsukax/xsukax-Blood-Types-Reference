Understanding Blood Types

Blood type is determined by the presence or absence of certain antigens on red blood cells. There are four main blood groups (A, B, AB, O) and two Rh factors (positive or negative), creating eight possible blood types.

🅰️

Type A

Has A antigens on red cells and anti-B antibodies in plasma. Can receive RBCs from A and O donors.

~34% globally*

🅱️

Type B

Has B antigens on red cells and anti-A antibodies in plasma. Can receive RBCs from B and O donors.

~9% globally*

🆎

Type AB

Has both A and B antigens, no naturally occurring anti-A or anti-B antibodies. Universal RBC recipient - can receive from all blood types. Note: Acquired antibodies can develop in certain clinical contexts.

~4% globally*

🅾️

Type O

Has no A or B antigens, both anti-A and anti-B antibodies present. Universal RBC donor - can donate to all blood types.

~53% globally*

*Percentages vary significantly by geographic region and ethnicity

Rh Factor (D Antigen)

➕ Rh Positive

Has the Rh D antigen on red blood cells. About 85% of people are Rh positive. Can receive Rh+ or Rh- RBCs.

➖ Rh Negative

Lacks the Rh D antigen. About 15% of people are Rh negative. Should only receive Rh- RBCs to prevent alloimmunization.

Note: "Rh negative" specifically refers to absence of the D antigen. The Rh system also includes other antigens (C, c, E, e) that may be clinically significant in certain situations.

🏥 Critical Safety Information

  • Crossmatching Required: All transfusions must be preceded by proper crossmatching (laboratory test mixing donor RBCs with recipient serum to detect reactions), even when ABO/Rh compatible
  • Antibody Screening: Testing for clinically significant antibodies (Kell, Duffy, Kidd, etc.) is mandatory
  • Universal Donor Context: O- is the safest emergency option until crossmatched blood becomes available
  • Professional Testing Only: Blood typing must be performed by certified laboratory personnel
  • Transfusion Reactions: All transfusions carry risks including transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO), transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI), and allergic reactions independent of compatibility
  • Special Populations: Neonatal and pediatric transfusions require specialized protocols and dosing
  • Autoimmune Conditions: Patients with autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) may require specialized transfusion support beyond standard compatibility testing
  • Informed Consent: Transfusion decisions require patient consent and discussion of risks, benefits, and alternatives

Emergency Transfusion Protocols

Note: The compatibility shown is for red blood cell transfusions only. Plasma transfusions follow reverse compatibility rules (e.g., AB plasma can be given to all blood types).

Emergency/Trauma: In life-threatening situations with unknown blood type, hospitals may use O- RBCs and AB plasma according to institutional massive transfusion protocols. In some centers, Rh+ blood may be used for Rh- patients in extreme emergencies with appropriate RhoGAM follow-up.

How Blood Type is Inherited

Blood type is inherited from both parents through ABO and Rh genes:

  • Type A: Genotype AA or AO
  • Type B: Genotype BB or BO
  • Type AB: Genotype AB
  • Type O: Genotype OO
  • Rh Factor: Dominant inheritance (Rh+ can be +/+ or +/-, Rh- is -/-)

Note: Without genetic testing, we cannot determine exact genotypes from phenotypes alone, which is why this calculator shows possibilities rather than certainties.

Rare Exceptions: This model does not account for rare genetic variants such as cis-AB inheritance, Bombay phenotype (Oh), chimerism, or genetic mutations that could result in unexpected blood types.

Blood Type Facts & Information

🌍

Global Distribution

Blood type distribution varies significantly by ethnicity and geography. O is most common worldwide (~45%), while AB is rarest (~4%). Asian populations have higher B frequencies than European populations.

🩸

Universal RBC Donor

O- blood is called the "universal donor" for red blood cells because it lacks A, B, and Rh antigens. Only 6-7% of people have O- blood, making it critically needed in emergencies until crossmatched blood is available.

💉

Universal RBC Recipient

AB+ individuals are universal RBC recipients because they have no anti-A, anti-B, or anti-Rh antibodies. However, they can only donate RBCs to other AB+ individuals. Makes up 3-4% of the population.

🔬

Discovery History

The ABO blood group system was discovered by Austrian scientist Karl Landsteiner in 1901. His work revolutionized transfusion medicine and earned him the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1930.

🤰

Rh Disease Prevention

Rh incompatibility between Rh- mothers and Rh+ babies can cause hemolytic disease of the newborn. Rh- mothers may receive anti-D immunoglobulin (RhoGAM) during pregnancy to prevent maternal antibody formation.

🧬

Beyond ABO & Rh

There are over 30 other blood group systems beyond ABO and Rh, including Kell, Duffy, Kidd, and MNS. These systems contain hundreds of antigens that can affect transfusion compatibility and require antibody screening. Additionally, HLA (human leukocyte antigen) antibodies can cause platelet refractoriness, requiring HLA-matched platelets rather than just ABO matching.

🏥 Blood Donation Importance

Blood banks constantly need donations, particularly O- and O+ blood which are used most frequently in emergencies. Regular blood donation is safe and can save multiple lives per donation.

Impact: One whole blood donation can save up to three lives through component separation (red cells, platelets, plasma).
